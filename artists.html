The people behind the paint

Meet the Artists

Four extraordinary minds. Four different ways of seeing the world.

1853–1890 · Dutch

Vincent van Gogh

Van Gogh developed a deeply personal language of energetic marks and intense colour in the final decade of his life. He began as a self-taught artist and went on to make landscapes, portraits, still lifes, and night scenes feel astonishingly immediate.

His use of contrasting colours and rhythmic brushwork influenced generations of modern artists, who saw in his work a new permission to make feeling visible.

  • The Starry Night
  • Sunflowers
  • Bedroom in Arles

1881–1973 · Spanish

Pablo Picasso

Picasso was a restless experimenter whose early naturalism gave way to the radical fractured viewpoints of Cubism. Across painting, sculpture, printmaking, collage, and ceramics, he kept asking how form could be remade.

His changing styles altered the course of modern art, inviting viewers to see a face, a room, or a history from more than one angle at once.

  • Guernica
  • Les Demoiselles d’Avignon
  • The Weeping Woman

1840–1926 · French

Claude Monet

Monet helped define Impressionism by painting changing light, air, water, and weather with a directness that feels fresh more than a century later. He often returned to the same subject at different times of day and seasons.

His late garden paintings, especially the Water Lilies series, turn observation into an almost immersive experience of reflected colour and atmosphere.

  • Water Lilies
  • Impression, Sunrise
  • Haystacks

1452–1519 · Italian

Leonardo da Vinci

Leonardo’s art grew from sustained observation. In Renaissance Florence and Milan, he studied anatomy, plants, water, optics, and expression, bringing that curiosity to remarkably balanced compositions.

His paintings are few in number, but their soft transitions of light and carefully observed human presence became touchstones for artists for centuries.

  • Mona Lisa
  • The Last Supper
  • Lady with an Ermine

Different Visions. One World of Art.

From Renaissance observation to Impressionist light, from expressive colour to Cubist fracture, each artist changed the questions viewers could ask of an image. Together, their work reminds us that art history is a living conversation across time.
